A LITTLE BIOGRAPHY OF DOZYCAT · ONGOING

In an age where everyone is about
to be replaced, I just wanted to live lighter.

Negativity everywhere, targets always rising. I didn't want to run faster — I wanted to sleep earlier, drink more water, have somewhere to put a bad day, and someone to remember what I forget. I knew all this. I just couldn't do it.

So I built myself a cat.
It doesn't do my work: other AIs do the work — this one takes care of me.

Scroll for its story ↓
CHAPTER ONE

It moved into the corner of my desktop

There was no ceremony the day it arrived. It just settled in the bottom-right corner of the screen, rising and falling with its breath, blinking now and then.

I soon realized the cat itself is the energy gauge: the longer I sit, the droopier it gets; when I stay up late, it sleeps badly too; when I come back from a walk, its ears perk up. The numbers stay hidden — only when I hover over it do the two energy bars come out to say hello.

Design principle: the cat is the gauge. Its body and face show your state; numbers appear only on hover and in the menu bar.
CHAPTER TWO

It remembers what I forget

When did that toothache start? Which folder has the photo with Lin? These questions used to end in "never mind."

Now I press ⌥ Space and just ask. It flips through its little notebook — my confidings, time notes, local files — lays the clues out one by one, and adds: want me to set a follow-up reminder?

Design principle: memory and file search run entirely on your machine. Nothing is uploaded.
CHAPTER THREE

It hears me out

The day my proposal got rejected for the third time, I clicked on it and said just one thing: "ugh."

It didn't run a retrospective. It didn't recite a pep talk. It just said: don't think about it for now — you haven't had water since noon. Later, in its notebook, I found one small line: this project has been draining you lately.

Some things only need to be remembered.

Design principle: moments worth keeping slip quietly into your story. It never makes a show of it.
CHAPTER FOUR

It keeps my books

It manages my energy like an asset: a calendar for the long run, candlesticks for today, and the recharge list as my "buy" orders.

A 20-minute evening walk: done 23 times, +12 on average. Calling mom: +15. It even schedules tomorrow's recharges in advance, then adds: on this plan, body energy closes tomorrow around 81.

Design principle: treat energy as an asset you manage — own the drains, schedule the recharges.
CHAPTER FIVE

It writes me

Then I discovered it was writing a book. About me. Data as material, days as chapters, a new installment on the first of every month.

Every chapter in the table of contents carries its margin note. On "The Wisdom Tooth": "we'll see" has now lasted two months; this cat remains watchful. On update day it hands the new chapter to my desktop and asks: read it now, or before bed?

Design principle: you're busy living the days — it writes them down for you.
INTERLUDE

Its eight moods

Dozing is the default. Stay up late and it worries; run empty and it flattens out; ignore it for hours and it tilts its head at you.
You don't need the numbers to know how you're doing — it acts you out.

EPILOGUE

At the end of the day,
it winds down with me.

After 11pm the screen dims, gently. It counts out the three small things I managed today, breathes with me for three minutes, and says goodnight. In the morning, it wakes me softly.
